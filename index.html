The case

Why conventional offsetting falls short.

Conventional carbon offsetting was not designed to meet today’s operational or regulatory realities. External projects are disconnected from your value chain, difficult to verify, increasingly scrutinized by regulators, and exposed to integrity and permanence risks.

Meanwhile your largest emissions, risks and vulnerabilities sit in Scope 3 supply chains — where a credit bought somewhere else delivers nothing operationally.

So the question we start from is not how to generate carbon credits that might benefit operations. It is the inverse: how can a supply-chain intervention that reduces real risk and cost also generate credible climate outcomes?

Design principles

How we design a nature-based investment.

We call this approach the Dual Value Model: nature-based investments justified by supply-chain economics first, with verified climate outcomes as a durable co-product. The principles below work together as a system, so that each makes the others stronger and helps correct the failures you see in many conventional carbon markets.

Supply-chain value first

Every project is designed to deliver documented supply-chain benefit as the primary investment justification, with sustainability outcomes developed as additional value. The finance case should stand on operational grounds.

Supply-chain driven location

Projects are designed to sit inside your value chain, not at sites selected opportunistically for credit generation. That is what lets a reduction address your own sourcing vulnerabilities.

Insetting eligibility

Because the project sits within your Scope 3 boundary, the reduction can count toward your own inventory when it is measured, attributed and documented to the standard described below.

Community co-ownership

Projects are designed for community co-ownership — equity and a governance role, not only benefit payments. Aligned incentives support permanence in ways contracts alone do not, and reduce the social-license conflicts that have undermined projects and reputations.

Long-term partnership

Structured as 20 to 30-year partnerships, giving projects stable financing independent of spot markets and giving buyers long-term visibility of supply.

Evidence from inception

The approach is designed to support tightening disclosure and due-diligence expectations, including CSRD, EUDR and SBTi FLAG, by generating auditable evidence from the start, rather than retrofitting disclosure later.

Insetting vs offsetting

Only one of them moves your Scope 3 number.

This is the distinction that decides which tool fits which target — and it is the one most often gotten wrong in a committee paper.

01

Offsetting

Buying credits generated outside your value chain and retiring them against residual emissions. The reduction happens elsewhere, financed by you. It does not lower your inventory number.

02

Insetting

Investing in reductions inside your own value chain, typically with suppliers, where the reduction is linked to what you actually buy. Properly measured and attributed, it lowers the inventory itself.

03

Both, in sequence

Most companies with material supply-chain exposure need both, in different proportions, over time. Insetting takes longer to stand up but changes the number permanently.

The practical consequence. If a science-based target requires an absolute reduction in Scope 3 emissions, insetting moves you toward it and offsetting does not — and it counts only when the intervention sits with an entity in your value chain, the reduction is measured against a defensible baseline, it is attributed to your share of that supplier’s output rather than double-counted, and the documentation survives audit.

Questions

Nature-based solutions, answered.

What are nature-based solutions in a carbon context?

They are projects that use natural systems — forests, soils, wetlands, agricultural land — to reduce or remove greenhouse gas emissions, while also delivering benefits to the people and ecosystems around them. In a corporate context the useful distinction is not the habitat but the boundary: whether the project sits inside your value chain or outside it.

How is this different from buying carbon offsets?

Offsetting means buying credits generated outside your value chain and retiring them against residual emissions, so the reduction happens elsewhere. A nature-based investment designed into your own supply chain is insetting: the reduction occurs inside your Scope 3 boundary and, properly measured and attributed, lowers your inventory number rather than sitting beside it.

Will this count toward our Scope 3 target?

It can, when four conditions hold: the intervention sits with an entity in your value chain, the reduction is measured against a defensible baseline, it is attributed to your share of that supplier’s output rather than double-counted with other buyers, and the documentation survives audit. Where those do not hold, the reduction is still useful but it belongs on a different ledger.

What is the most common way insetting goes wrong?

Double counting. If your supplier sells the same reduction as a credit on the voluntary market and also reports it to you as a Scope 3 reduction, the arithmetic breaks. It has to be addressed deliberately — usually by contractual provisions that stop the supplier selling the reduction twice.

Why do you build community co-ownership into projects?

Because permanence depends on it. When projects are designed so that communities hold equity and a governance role rather than only benefit payments, the incentives to protect the project are aligned in a way contracts alone cannot match. It also reduces the social-license conflicts that have undermined both projects and corporate reputations.

Why are the agreements 20 to 30 years long?

Long-dated partnerships give projects the stable financing that quality requires and take them off the spot market. For buyers, they provide long-term visibility of supply rather than repricing every year.

Which regulations does this help with?

The approach is designed to support tightening disclosure and due-diligence expectations, including CSRD, EUDR and SBTi FLAG, by generating auditable evidence from the start of a project rather than retrofitting disclosure later. It does not replace your own reporting obligations, and we do not provide assurance or audit opinions.

We are not ready for a multi-year project. What then?

Then this is probably the wrong tool today. Measure your footprint first. If you need to address emissions this year, sourcing verified credits is the faster route, and we can help with that.
